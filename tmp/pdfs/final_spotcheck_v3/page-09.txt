5 DISCUSSION
Our results qualify the reassurance that low CoT controllability (as measured by current bench-
marks) can provide about our trust in the reliability of CoT monitoring. Two of our three addition
tasks and all three stylistic modification tasks impose constraints from the beginning of the reason-
ing trace. Their scores therefore depend partly on whether models can depart from entrenched open-
ings. One possible explanation is that a noncompliant opening establishes a pattern that subsequent
generation continues, despite the control instruction. However, adding misleading information or
obscuring relevant reasoning does not require changing a conventional opening. Sensitivity to this
opening bottleneck may therefore cause current benchmarks to understate the control models can
exercise later in their traces. We argue that evaluations intended to measure these risks should dis-
tinguish the ability to initiate a prescribed format from the ability to selectively alter what reasoning
conveys.
The monitorability results further complicate this picture of controllability as a safety indicator.
Rather than a uniform negative relationship between controllability and monitorability, we find as-
sociations that vary across control tasks and evaluations: addition and style tasks show positive
associations in most evaluations, while suppression tasks show both positive and negative associa-
tions. However, these associations do not establish that addition and style capabilities are benign,
or that suppression proxies directly measure concealment. The positive associations observed for
some addition and style tasks are compatible with models producing informative reasoning in ordi-
nary settings while retaining the ability to make it misleading under different incentives. Likewise,
negative associations for suppression do not establish that avoiding characters or phrases measures
the ability to conceal relevant reasoning. This uncertainty concerns both sides of the comparison:
monitorability evaluations are themselves proxies, and success at identifying a cue, theorem, or out-
come property does not establish that a monitor can reliably detect strategically omitted or fabricated
information in a different setting. Consequently, these correlations should inform the validation of
both evaluation suites, rather than be treated as direct relationships between control capabilities and
the reliability of oversight. Our results therefore challenge a uniform inverse relationship between
controllability and monitorability, without resolving how particular control capabilities contribute to
intentional monitor evasion. Thus, the relevant question for future work is which forms of control
enable models to withhold or misrepresent information that a monitor needs.
Our conclusions are limited by the number and diversity of models and control tasks studied, as
well as uncertainty in their measured scores. Several monitorability evaluations place many models
near the floor or ceiling, leaving limited separation between them. When these differences are small
relative to evaluation uncertainty, rank correlations can depend on orderings that the measurements
do not reliably distinguish. The cross-model associations we report are therefore descriptive. These
limitations motivate reporting control tasks separately and validating them against deliberate con-
cealment and misleading disclosure. Low controllability is most informative as a safety indicator
when we understand why a model fails the task and whether that failure actually limits its ability to
evade oversight.
