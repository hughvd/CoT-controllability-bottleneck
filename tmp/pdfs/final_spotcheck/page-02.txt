In this work, we aim to understand the mechanism underlying reduced controllability in LLMs as
well as its relation to monitorability. To this end, we adopt controllability tasks from Kwon et al.
(2026) and Chen et al. (2026), and distinguish between suppressive controllability (avoiding or omit-
ting information), additive controllability (adding information), and stylistic controllability (trans-
forming information) following Chen et al. (2026). For monitorability, we use the evaluations from
Guan et al. (2025), targeting three different types of monitorability. We evaluate models from three
different families (Team, 2025; Olmo et al., 2025; GLM-5-Team et al., 2026) ranging from 14B to
753B parameters on their ability to follow control instructions while answering questions from five
different benchmarks. To examine whether the resulting distinctions extend across models, we ad-
ditionally measure associations among control tasks and between controllability and monitorability
in a separate twelve-model panel. We report three main findings:
•Low controllability is partly an initiation problem: models assign most of the proba-
bility mass in the first few tokens after the opening think tag to highly templated opening
phrases (see Figure 1), leading to an entropy collapse. When intervening on these tokens,
either by prefilling tokens compliant with the control instruction, or by banning frequent
the model’s most frequent opener, controllability can partly but significantly recover.
•Additive and suppressive controllability likely underlie distinct mechanisms: token in-
terventions mainly increase additive and not suppressive controllability. Moreover, these
control types behave as different capabilities: correlative analysis shows that a model per-
forming well on additive or style transform types tend to do well on other additive and style
transform types but not suppression types, and similarly performance on suppressive types
is only predictive of performance on other suppressive types.
•Additive and suppressive controllability show different relationships with monitora-
bility: while suppressive controllability can sometimes correlate negatively with moni-
torability, additive controllability mainly correlates positively with monitorability. This
indicates that aggregate measures of controllability may obscure the patterns required for
tracking monitorability.
Our findings refine the role of CoT controllability in monitorability. Current evaluations of addi-
tive and stylistic controllability may be weak proxies for the monitorability-relevant control they
are intended to measure. These forms of controllability can be partially recovered through simple
interventions on the first few tokens, and they tend to be associated with increased monitorabil-
ity. This suggests these tasks might not capture a model’s ability to introduce misleading content
that evades oversight. Second, suppressive controllability appears to be a more plausible proxy for
monitorability-relevant control, as it is the only form of controllability that shows negative associa-
tions with monitorability in some settings. Besides suggesting that suppressive controllability may
depend on distinct mechanisms, our findings also indicate suppressive evaluations should not be
collapsed into aggregate controllability measures. We therefore recommend that future work report
